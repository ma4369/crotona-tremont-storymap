01 / The place

Where we live shapes what we breathe.

Crotona–Tremont is a Bronx neighborhood grouping used by the New York City Environment & Health Data Portal. Here, housing quality, financial pressures, and exposure to environmental triggers intersect with childhood asthma.

Offline-friendly orientation diagram showing the approximate location of Crotona–Tremont within the Bronx. Not to scale and not an official neighborhood boundary or address-level exposure map. NYC reporting area includes ZIP codes 10453, 10457, and 10460. For detailed geography, use the linked NYC Health Data Portal report.

Why this neighborhood?

The NYC Health Data Portal reports a high childhood asthma emergency department visit rate here compared with the group's two other selected neighborhoods. The comparison raises an important question: what roles might housing conditions, socioeconomic circumstances, and access to care play?

Neighborhood rates can reveal inequities and guide investigation. They cannot tell us which individual children experienced a specific housing problem.

03 / Housing & resources

The conditions behind the statistics.

Homes with water leaks, pest infestation, and maintenance problems may contain allergens and other asthma triggers. Socioeconomic conditions can also shape a family's housing options and ability to address hazards.

Housing exposures to investigate

Moisture & mold

Water intrusion and dampness can contribute to indoor environmental conditions relevant to asthma.

Pests & allergens

Cockroaches and rodents can introduce allergens that aggravate asthma symptoms.

Maintenance & repairs

Structural disrepair may make exposure reduction more difficult for families.

Careful interpretation

The poverty, rent burden, and age figures are ACS survey-based community estimates, not counts of children who visited an emergency department.

Likewise, this page does not claim that 33.9% of children with asthma live in poverty, or that a given household with a leak experienced an asthma-related ED visit.

04 / Research & interpretation

What does the evidence suggest?

Housing quality

Northridge et al. (2010) reported associations between water leaks, cockroaches, rats, and childhood asthma in NYC. This supports examining housing hazards as possible contributors—not assuming causation from our neighborhood comparison.

Indoor allergens

Olmedo et al. (2011) found differences in cockroach and mouse allergen exposures across NYC neighborhoods. These exposures provide possible pathways connecting housing environments and asthma.

Community interventions

López et al. (2015) evaluated a combined housing repair, education, and case-management program in East Harlem and reported improvements among participants. The small uncontrolled evaluation cannot isolate which element caused the change.

Public health takeaway: addressing housing maintenance, reducing indoor allergens, and supporting consistent asthma care are plausible intervention priorities. Our neighborhood-level comparison identifies a reason to act and study further, not proof that one exposure explains the gap.

What the data cannot tell us

  1. No individual-level causation. We cannot determine whether children who needed emergency asthma care lived in homes with leaks or pests. Doing so would risk an ecological fallacy.
  2. Different measurement windows. The ED visit rates are for 2023 while displayed socioeconomic data summarize ACS 2019–2023. Not all measures reflect the same period.
  3. Uncertainty and geographic units. This presentation does not yet display uncertainty intervals for the ED rates; results may also differ with alternative neighborhood boundaries.
  4. Unmeasured influences. Outdoor pollution, healthcare access, household exposures, and demographic composition may contribute to the observed patterns.
